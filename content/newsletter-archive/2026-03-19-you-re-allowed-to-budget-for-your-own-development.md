Can I tell you about something that almost every scientist misses when writing
a funding proposal?

It's not the science. It's not the methodology. It's not even the impact
section (though yes, that too).

It's the budget where you are actually allowed to invest in yourself.

Most proposals have a section for project management, coordination, or career
development activities. It goes by different names depending on the funding
scheme, but it is almost always there. And almost always, scientists either
leave it vague, fill it with generic descriptions, or don't use it at all.

Here's what you could put there instead: **leadership training.
Mentorship. One-on-one coaching. Skills workshops** that are directly
relevant to executing the project you are proposing.

And yes — **AI literacy training counts**.

Think about it this way. Your host institution will offer general training. It
has to — those sessions are designed for everyone in the department, across all
levels, across all disciplines. That is their job. But it is your job to invest
in the specific skills that will make *you* a better researcher and a
better PI on *this* project.

The funding is there. **You just have to plan it in.**

There is also something else happening when you do this well. A proposal that
budgets thoughtfully for professional development sends a signal to reviewers:
this person is thinking beyond the next three years. They understand that
executing ambitious research requires building a capable, well-supported team —
and that starts with the PI.

That is the kind of long-term thinking that evaluators notice.

The key is how you phrase it. "Career development" that reads as a perk will
not land well. **Career development that is clearly tied to project
delivery** — that is a completely different conversation. And that is
exactly where I can help.

Keep this in mind when you are writing your next grant proposal. Share this
email with your science besties.
