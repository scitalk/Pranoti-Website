**What is article 4?**

Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires every organisation that builds or uses AI systems to ensure their staff have a sufficient level of AI literacy. That's not a recommendation or a best-practice guideline — it's an enforceable obligation, and it applies to essentially every research institute on the continent.

Most researchers I speak to are vaguely aware of this. Far fewer know what to actually do about it.

**The tricky part for research institutes?** You're sitting at a dual compliance point. If your team is developing an ML model, you're an AI *provider*. If your grants office is using an LLM to draft funding reports, you're an AI *deployer*. Article 4 obligations come at you from both directions.

Add to that the fact that a particle physicist using ML for data analysis has completely different literacy needs from an administrator using a document assistant — and one-size-fits-all training won't cut it.

**So what does compliance actually look like?**

Three practical steps:

**Map your AI use first.** Before any training, you need to know which AI tools are in use, by whom, and in what context. Many institutes find this picture is broader than expected — tools adopted quietly at team level, long before any central strategy was in place.

**Differentiate by role.** A tiered approach works best: foundational awareness for all staff, plus role-specific competence training for those using AI directly in their work.

**Document and iterate.** The AI landscape changes faster than any training programme. What matters for compliance is an active, documented approach that gets reviewed and updated. The European Commission's Living Repository of AI Literacy Practices is specifically designed to help with this.

The good news: compliance doesn't require perfection. It requires intention, documentation, and progress.
