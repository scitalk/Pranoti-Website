## In Other Words

A not-yet-published project (from his master's) showing catalytic chemistry runs very differently inside nanoscale mesoporous 'straws', e.g. hydrogen formed and consumed in the same confined nanosphere so it can't escape.

## Key Takeaways

- Catalysis sits in almost every chemical product, so small tweaks to a catalyst change a whole process, less energy, higher selectivity, less waste; this is the lever green chemistry pulls.
- Pharma is the worst offender (~90% waste, 10% product) because molecules need many low-yield steps, and most of that waste is solvent, which chemists routinely ignore.
- Concrete wins: making rose-oxide fragrance (multi-ton scale) via a photochemical step (light is abundant and safer than heat), and making amines in one step that reincorporates fragments that would otherwise be waste (atom economy).
- Heterogeneous catalysis (sand-in-water, not salt-in-water) lets you filter or settle out scarce, toxic metal catalysts so they don't end up in the product.
- Confinement effect: molecules in spaces near their own size constantly hit the walls and each other and so react differently, he trapped in-situ-generated hydrogen in tiny nanospheres so it's consumed before it escapes, and wrote a COVID-lockdown review on using confinement to make chemistry greener.
- The green-chemistry community is unusually tight-knit and welcoming, and courses/summer schools now exist where they didn't in his undergrad; he came to chemistry wanting to teach and values science's global, people-centred side. Three wishes: more LGBTQ, women and BIPOC people in STEM.

## About the guest

Julio Terra is a Post-doctoral Associate at EPFL in Switzerland, where he designs nanostructures with controlled composition, size and shape to make catalysts for green chemistry. In his PhD at McGill University, he trapped a catalyst inside hollow silica shells to study confined chemistry. He obtained his BSc and MSc in Chemistry in Brazil, with exchanges at the University of Toronto and McGill.
