Jessica Henty-Ridilla studies the cytoskeleton, which she describes as the bones of your cell. In her lab that means two proteins, actin and microtubules, which are constantly built up and taken apart, like Lego. She wants to know how the two coordinate, and what happens when that regulation goes wrong in diseases such as ALS.

## About the guest

Jessica Henty-Ridilla was an assistant professor at SUNY Upstate Medical University in Syracuse, New York, when we recorded this episode in 2022. Her [faculty profile](https://www.upstate.edu/cdb/education/faculty.php?empID=ridillaj) now lists her as an associate professor in Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, and in 2024 her lab received a [$2.2 million grant renewal](https://www.upstate.edu/news/articles/2024/2024-08-22-lab.php) to continue its protein research. Her lab combines cell biology, genetics, advanced microscopy and test-tube reconstitution of the cytoskeleton.

## Why it matters

Actin and microtubules decide a cell's shape, how it moves and how it divides, and Jessica explains that their regulation goes wrong in ALS, in cancer and in a lot of other disorders. Profilin ties the two together directly: it adjusts how both grow, and mutations in its gene cause ALS. Her lab starts from the basic question of how the two parts of the cytoskeleton talk to each other, then asks how proteins carrying disease mutations change that. As Jessica puts it, working out how a protein does what it does matters on a very basic level, and it could matter for making a drug to treat patients.

## Terms explained

In the guest's own words, with a link to the moment they explain it.

- **Cytoskeleton:** the "bones" of the cell. In Jessica's lab that means actin and microtubules, the proteins that dictate a cell's shape, how it moves and how it divides. *(Jessica at [02:38](https://youtu.be/7VY8qG_PqiY?t=158))*
- **Profilin:** a regulating protein that adjusts how both actin and microtubules grow. Mutations in its gene cause ALS. *(Jessica at [09:44](https://youtu.be/7VY8qG_PqiY?t=584))*
- **TIRF microscopy:** Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence. A reflected laser creates an evanescent wave that lights up only about 100 nanometres of the sample next to the cover slip, so images look crisp and clear. *(Jessica at [20:46](https://youtu.be/7VY8qG_PqiY?t=1246))*
