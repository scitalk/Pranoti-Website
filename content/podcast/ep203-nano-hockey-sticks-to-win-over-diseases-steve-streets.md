          images: ["/images/podcasts/takeover/steven-street/FvYutz1aQAEzpKE.webp"]
        - text: "There are MANY factors that affect how good a drug delivery system is. Some of the main ones are: particle size, shape, hardness, surface charge, surface chemistry, and architecture. Most delivery systems are nanoscopic, so they are very small!"
        - text: "How small you ask? Well, typically they are ~100nm, which is 1000x smaller than the width of a human hair. To put that another way, they are ~100x bigger than the speed your hair grows every second! At this scale, controlling the size and shape of objects is hard."
        - text: "This size is too small to physically manipulate, though ‘top-down’ methods such as lithography are used to try and ‘cut’ nanoparticles out! (e.g. look up DeSimone’s PRINT). This size is also much bigger than the molecular world. For example, a glucose molecule is ~1nm wide!"
          images: ["/images/podcasts/takeover/steven-street/FvYu5d9aAAESG4L.webp"]
        - text: "So, we have to try ‘bottom-up’ processes, which aim to make larger structures from molecular building blocks. This is challenging as you have to rely on fundamental physics to get the building blocks to ‘self-assemble’ into the shape you want."
        - text: "A sphere has the lowest volume out of any shape, so when these building blocks ‘self-assemble’, they usually form spheres to minimize their energy and avoid unwanted interactions. Making non-spherical nanomaterials is therefore a big challenge!"
        - text: "Both the AAV viruses and LNPs that are mentioned above are spherical or quasi-spherical. One of the aims of my research is to make non-spherical delivery systems to see if other shapes are better at delivering medicines!"
        - text: "But, there are limited methods for making non-spherical nanomaterials, and making them all the same size and shape is hard. This is important for medicine though! The study of using nanoparticles in medicine is called ‘nanomedicine’"

## In Other Words

Making tiny non-spherical block-copolymer 'sticks' (nanofibers via crystallisation-driven self-assembly) functionalised to bind DNA on their surface, the first non-spherical 'micelleplex', as gene-delivery vehicles, tested by making brain-cancer cells glow with firefly luciferase.

## In this episode

- Why everything at the nanoscale wants to be a sphere, and how Steve makes polymer nanofibres, or tiny sticks, instead
- The difference between top-down and bottom-up nanomaterials, explained with stone and clay
- His path from a most improved student award and a job testing soil for pesticides to a PhD on sugar-based anti-cancer compounds in Bristol, then a move to the University of Victoria
- Why getting medicines to the right place in the body, such as the brain, is so hard
- The project he is proudest of: nanofibres that carry DNA on the outside, like hair caught in a hairbrush, tested by making brain cancer cells glow
- His three wishes: a less wasteful funding system, a public that understands chemistry better, and a better quality of life for scientists in academia

## Key Takeaways

- Postdoc at University of Victoria making precision non-spherical nanomaterials (10 nm–10 µm), because at the nanoscale everything wants to be a sphere.
- Career built on taking opportunities: a 'most improved chemistry student' award led to an industry job, then a Bristol CDT PhD where students pick their own project.
- Shifted from small-molecule anti-cancer sugars to polymer drug delivery because getting medicine to the right place (e.g. the brain) is the real bottleneck.
- Values the mental workout, freedom to choose directions, and better work-life balance in his group.
- Three wishes: a streamlined funding system, better public understanding of chemistry, and Silicon-Valley-style quality of life in academia.
