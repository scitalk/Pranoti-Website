**Engineered living materials** could soon give buildings the ability to heal their own cracks, filter city air and block UV, all without a drop of paint. On the latest [Under the Microscope Podcast](https://thesciencetalk.com/under-the-microscope/), Anna Sandak, Head of the Materials Group at the University of Primorska, explains how she embeds living fungi, algae and bacteria into building coatings. The result: facades that behave more like organisms than surfaces.

### What are engineered living materials?

Anna’s background is in biology, not architecture. That’s precisely why her **living fungal coatings** work differently to conventional building materials. Instead of adding a protective layer once and hoping it lasts, her coatings stay biologically active. They **self-heal scratches**, absorb air pollutants, and produce natural UV protection through melanin, the same pigment that protects human skin.

## Key takeaways

**Buildings become active, not just static.** Anna’s coatings are designed to reduce the building sector’s carbon footprint throughout its operational life, not only during construction.

**Good fungi, not mushrooms.** The fungal biofilms are microscopic and selected to outcompete harmful organisms, much like a healthy microbiome protects the human body.

**Aesthetics and sustainability go hand in hand.** The ARCHI-Biome “tattoo” technology lets architects print high-resolution designs that also clean air and resist weathering.

**Cross-disciplinary teams accelerate innovation.** Anna’s EIC Pathfinder project sits inside a ten-project portfolio on engineered living materials, spanning architecture to human health, all working together on regulatory and market-readiness questions.

## About the guest

Anna Sandak leads the Materials Group at the [University of Primorska (formerly InnoRenew CoE)](https://innorenew.eu/) in Slovenia. She holds an ERC Consolidator grant for ARCHI-SKIN and leads Slovenia’s contribution to the EIC Pathfinder project REMEDY, alongside a national ARIS-funded project sending fungal biofilms into space.
