Claire Dancer says ceramics get overlooked because people think they are brittle, yet we use them all the time, from mugs and plates to light fittings and plug sockets. Making them traditionally means firing a powder in a furnace well above 1,000 °C for a long time. Her group works on new ways to make ceramics at much lower temperatures and with far less energy.

## About the guest

Claire Dancer is a Reader at WMG, University of Warwick, in the UK, where she has led a ceramic processing group since 2013. She grew up near Cardiff, studied natural sciences and materials science at Cambridge, and did her PhD at Oxford on magnesium diboride, a superconductor. Postdocs on ceramic armour and on metamaterials followed. According to her [Warwick profile](https://warwick.ac.uk/wie/getinvolved/membersandfellows/fellows-about/foundation/), she also leads outreach and engagement for the UK Metamaterials Network.

Claire Dancer curated the Real Scientists Nano Twitter account (@RealSci_Nano) from December 5 to December 12 2022. [Read all of Claire's tweets from that week](/realsci-nano-tweets/#g102).

## Why it matters

Claire explains that 90% of the energy in a ceramic's lifetime goes into making it, and that without new processes the ceramics industry can't hit its emissions targets. Switching old gas kilns to electricity and renewables helps, but on its own it doesn't bring the energy use down enough. Lower temperatures also matter for devices. Solid-state batteries need dense ceramic layers, but sintering temperatures are so high that people glue ceramic particles together with polymer and lose the properties they wanted. Her group pushes temperatures below the melting points of metals and even polymers. The next challenge is scale: cold sintering needs pressure, and the goal is making pouch cells on a production line, not one coin cell at a time.

## Terms explained

In the guest's own words, with a link to the moment they explain it.

- **Conventional ceramic processing:** shape a powder into a pellet, then fire it in a furnace well above 1,000 °C (around 1,600 °C for aluminium oxide) so it densifies by solid-state diffusion. *(Claire at [07:07](https://youtu.be/O4OnP-0ZTCQ?t=427))*
- **Flash sintering:** connecting electrodes to the ceramic and passing an electric field through it while heating the furnace to a more modest temperature, so the energy goes straight into the sample. It often gives a flash of light. *(Claire at [46:33](https://youtu.be/O4OnP-0ZTCQ?t=2793))*
- **Cold sintering:** adding a small amount of liquid and applying pressure at 100 to 300 °C, so a little material dissolves and the particles rearrange and fuse. *(Claire at [48:52](https://youtu.be/O4OnP-0ZTCQ?t=2932))*
