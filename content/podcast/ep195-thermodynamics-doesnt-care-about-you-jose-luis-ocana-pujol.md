Jose Ocaña-Pujol works on optical metamaterials, materials whose properties come from their geometry rather than their chemistry. His group makes them in about an hour instead of days, and he studies whether they hold up at high temperatures. He also learned, after a swim in a cold Lake Zurich, that thermodynamics always wins.

## About the guest

Jose Luis Ocaña-Pujol was a PhD student at ETH Zurich in Switzerland when we recorded this episode in 2022. He studied nanoscience at the Autonomous University of Barcelona, then took a master's in Science for Energy and Sustainability in Amsterdam, which included research at AMOLF and a part-time job in energy analysis. His 2022 paper with colleagues at ETH Zurich, [*Strain-Driven Thermal and Optical Instability in Silver/Amorphous-Silicon Hyperbolic Metamaterials*](https://doi.org/10.1002/adom.202201749), was published in *Advanced Optical Materials*.

## Why it matters

The first metamaterials were made top-down, which was difficult and time-consuming. Jose's group designs metamaterials that can be made in minutes or hours rather than days, and that hold up at the high temperatures needed for applications such as hyperthermia and thermophotovoltaics. His paper showed two things. Fast deposition gives rough interfaces, and that disorder actually improved how much light the material absorbs. And when the layers did break down, the cause was stress from the different thermal expansion of the materials, which multilayer theory usually overlooks. Changing the stacking order changed where the breakdown started.

## Terms explained

In the guest's own words, with a link to the moment they explain it.

- **Metamaterial:** a material whose properties come from its geometry, the shapes and patterns it is built from, rather than its chemistry. This can give properties not normally found in nature. *(Jose at [03:06](https://youtu.be/uzyM7aztu6U?t=186))*
- **Feature size:** for a metamaterial to work with visible light (about 500 to 800 nanometres), its features need to be at least about five times smaller than the wavelength. *(Jose at [08:36](https://youtu.be/uzyM7aztu6U?t=516))*
- **Focused ion beam (FIB) cross-section:** sending ions to cut through a sample, like cutting a cake, so you can look at the layer structure inside. *(Jose at [20:01](https://youtu.be/uzyM7aztu6U?t=1201))*
