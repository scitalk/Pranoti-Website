The European Commission publishes a record of every ERC grant it has ever awarded. Pull ten years of it, 11,953 grants and €21.49bn across call years 2016 to 2025, and one thing jumps out before any of the funding patterns do.

The United Kingdom disappears.

| Call year | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK, all grant types | 183 | 178 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 200 | 164 |
| Switzerland, core grants | 71 | 70 | 3 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 62 |

The first time you see this, it looks like a broken export. It is not. It is the single cleanest picture I have seen of what association status actually costs, because it is not an estimate or a projection. It is the award record itself.

## What the gap is

The dates line up exactly with the Commission's own published position.

The UK [is associated to Horizon Europe as of 1 January 2024](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/strategy/strategy-research-and-innovation/europe-world/international-cooperation/association-horizon-europe/united-kingdom_en), following the EU-UK Joint Statement of 7 September 2023, with UK entities participating "from the 2024 work programme onwards." For the three years before that, the Commission's own wording is that UK entities "took part in calls for proposals for the years 2021, 2022 and 2023" under a transitional arrangement that let them "apply and be evaluated as other potential beneficiaries," with money coming from the UK Guarantee rather than the EU.

Switzerland's [association agreement was signed on 10 November 2025](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/strategy/strategy-research-and-innovation/europe-world/international-cooperation/association-horizon-europe/switzerland_en), with funding eligibility "for all calls from the 2025 budget onwards." Which is why Swiss grants reappear in call year 2025 and not before.

So the researchers did not stop existing, and in the UK's case many of them did not stop being funded. They stopped being funded *by the ERC*, and therefore stopped appearing in the record that everyone treats as the scoreboard of European research excellence.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Rankings, benchmarking exercises, institutional strategy documents and grant office target lists all get built on this dataset. For three years it was measuring political status as much as scientific merit, and nothing in the file says so.

## Who took the space

Between them, the UK and Switzerland held **24.1%** of ERC core grants (Starting, Consolidator and Advanced) across call years 2016 to 2020. During 2021 to 2023 they held essentially none. That quarter of the programme went somewhere.

Germany took the largest single slice. That is the unsurprising part.

## The part that is actually interesting

The UK came back in call year 2024 and immediately returned to its old level. 165 core grants, against a 2016-2020 average of 157 per year. Three years out, and the recovery took one call cycle.

So what happened to everyone else's gains?

| Country | 2016-2020 | 2021-2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 17.8% | 22.8% | 18.1% |
| France | 10.6% | 13.4% | 9.8% |
| Netherlands | 9.0% | 11.2% | 9.7% |
| Italy | 5.5% | 8.1% | **8.1%** |
| Spain | 5.5% | 7.3% | 6.6% |
| Sweden | 3.6% | 4.4% | **4.7%** |
| Austria | 3.4% | 4.3% | **4.3%** |
| Denmark | 2.4% | 3.5% | 3.0% |
| Norway | 1.7% | 2.4% | **2.3%** |

Germany, France and the Netherlands handed the share straight back the moment the UK returned. Italy, Sweden, Austria and Norway did not.

Italy is the clearest case. It went from 5.5% to 8.1% during the gap and stayed at 8.1% in 2024, with the UK back in the room and the total pool larger. Sweden ended higher in 2024 than at any point in the gap.

I do not think that is luck. The countries that gave the share back were already operating at their ceiling and simply absorbed overflow demand for three years. The countries that kept it appear to have used the window to build something: more applications, better internal support, stronger candidates entering the pipeline. A three-year gap in the competition is long enough for a research office to change how it works, and short enough that most institutions would treat it as weather rather than climate.

Whoever treated it as climate is now permanently better off.

## What this means if you are applying

Three practical things follow.

**Benchmarking against 2021-2023 is benchmarking against a distortion.** If your institutional strategy document cites country or institution shares from those call years, it is quoting a market with a quarter of the competition removed. Success rates from those years are not comparable to now, in either direction.

**The competition in 2024 onwards is genuinely harder than in the gap.** The pool of core grants grew from around 970 a year during the gap to 1,106 in 2024, but the UK's 165 came back on top of gains that several countries kept. More grants, more competitors, and the mid-sized national systems are stronger than they were in 2020.

**Switzerland's return is still working through.** Swiss entities are eligible from the 2025 budget onwards and record 62 core grants in call year 2025, against roughly 70 a year before the gap. If Switzerland recovers the way the UK did, the next two call cycles absorb another five to seven percent of the programme. If you are modelling your odds for 2027, that is not yet visible in any historical number you can look up.

## A note on reading this data

Two caveats, because they change what you can honestly say.

Call year 2025 contains no Advanced Grants in this export, since those results were not published when it was taken. Any 2025 figure above is Starting and Consolidator only where it says core, and undercounts total activity. I have kept 2025 out of the comparison tables for that reason and used 2024 as the post-return year.

Second, "share of grants" is not "quality of science." A country's share moves with how many applications it submits as much as with how many succeed. The ERC does not publish per-country application counts in this dataset, so nothing here separates a rise in success from a rise in volume. That is a real limit, and I would rather say so than dress a count up as a verdict.

What the data does show, without ambiguity, is that a policy decision removed a quarter of Europe's flagship research competition for three years, and that the countries which treated the gap as an opportunity are still holding the ground they took.
