> **Key takeaways**
> - CADA introduces four cloud sovereignty assurance levels for EU public procurement, each defined by four measurable criteria
> - Member States and EU entities must conduct a sovereignty risk assessment for every cloud use case — it is not optional
> - The compliance requirement cascades: organisations supplying public bodies will face the same questions indirectly
> - The four criteria are specific enough to begin an internal mapping exercise now, before formal guidance is published

The EU's [Cloud and AI Development Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cloud-and-ai-development-act) (CADA), proposed on 3 June 2026, introduces four cloud sovereignty tiers for public sector procurement — each defined by a specific set of criteria, each attached to a mandatory risk assessment process. It is the first time the EU has embedded a formal sovereignty classification system directly into procurement law. If your organisation buys cloud from any provider — and the vast majority of European organisations do so from non-EU providers — the tier you sit in is about to become a procurement compliance question, not a strategic preference.

## Why CADA exists: a market that has not self-corrected

The EU's cloud market has become structurally concentrated without reversing course. Three US hyperscalers together hold more than 70% of the EU cloud market. European providers, which held 29% of the market in 2017, had fallen to 15% by 2022 — and that share has not recovered in four years.

The Commission's Communication on European Tech Sovereignty (COM(2026) 503) frames this as both a market failure and a security issue. European dependency on non-EU cloud exposes the EU to "risks relating to foreign jurisdictional data reach and surveillance, creating material risks to public order." High market concentration and vendor lock-in by a few non-EU players, it states, "may reduce competition, slow down innovation and leave public administrations, businesses and citizens vulnerable to economic and security risks."

CADA is the regulatory response. Rather than restricting which providers public bodies may use, it creates a classification system for how sovereign a cloud service actually is — and makes that classification a requirement of procurement.

## CADA's four cloud sovereignty tiers: what the criteria cover

The Union cloud and AI sovereignty framework in CADA comprises four distinct assurance levels, structured as a progression from baseline to full sovereignty. The Commission defines these levels using four criteria:

| Criterion | What it covers | Questions to ask your provider |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------------------|
| **Service and software supply chain** | Who controls the technology stack and its software dependencies | Where is your software developed and maintained? Can you demonstrate EU-governed oversight? |
| **AI inference data** | Where and under whose jurisdiction data processed by AI services is handled | Where does inference occur? Which legal jurisdiction governs the processing? |
| **Infrastructure, assets and personnel** | Physical and legal geography of service delivery | Is your infrastructure EU-located? Are operational personnel subject to EU jurisdiction? |
| **Cybersecurity** | Alignment with EU security standards and risk management frameworks | What EU cybersecurity certifications do you hold or are actively working towards? |

The four levels scale from a baseline of data residency requirements at the lower end up to full supply chain control, EU-governed AI inference processing, EU-located infrastructure, and high cybersecurity assurance at the upper end. The specific criteria for each level will be defined through implementing legislation. The Commission has confirmed it will publish guidance to ensure a consistent approach across Member States.

**Member States and EU entities must conduct a sovereignty risk assessment to determine which level of assurance is necessary for each use case.** This is mandatory, not advisory. Public sector bodies will be expected to classify each cloud service they procure — communications platforms, data storage, AI tools, collaboration environments — against the tier framework.

Their procurement processes will need to reflect those classifications. CADA also aims to promote EU added value in public procurement of cloud and AI more broadly. The sovereignty assurance tiers are the mechanism by which that objective becomes a concrete, auditable requirement rather than a policy statement.

## The compliance cascade: who it affects and when

CADA is a proposal as of June 2026, moving through the European Parliament and Council. But two factors make this relevant now rather than in 2028.

The Commission has already adopted a Cloud Sovereignty Framework for its own procurement, setting eight concrete sovereignty objectives and minimum assurance levels for cloud providers serving Commission departments. Public sector bodies at Member State level are observing this as a direct reference point. Procurement language is already shifting ahead of CADA's formal adoption.

The second factor is the cascade. When a public body procures cloud at a given assurance level, every organisation supplying services to that body — contractors, research partners, technology vendors — faces indirect pressure to understand and document its own cloud sovereignty position. Requirements do not stay inside public administration; they move through supply chains.

If you have been working through [vendor lock-in risks in your AI and cloud infrastructure](/perspectives/enterprise-ai-vendor-lockin-europe-dependency-risk/), CADA adds a direct regulatory dimension to that exposure. The dependency risk was always there. The CADA sovereignty tiers are what turn it into a compliance classification.

## Start the mapping exercise: a five-step checklist

Formal guidance has not yet been published. But the four criteria are specific enough to begin an internal audit today.

1. **Map your cloud services** — list every platform your organisation uses for communications, data storage, AI tools, and collaboration
2. **Identify supply chain visibility** — for each service, document what you know about where the software is developed, maintained, and by whom
3. **Locate AI inference** — if your team uses AI tools, establish where data is processed and under which legal jurisdiction inference occurs
4. **Confirm infrastructure geography** — check whether your provider can demonstrate EU-located infrastructure and confirm that personnel with operational access are subject to EU jurisdiction
5. **Audit cybersecurity certifications** — identify which EU security certifications your primary cloud providers currently hold or are actively pursuing

None of these steps requires a formal assessment process to begin. An inventory mapped against the four criteria gives you the baseline from which a sovereignty risk assessment can be built once Commission guidance is published.

---

CADA's sovereignty tier framework is new. The market problem it addresses has been building for a decade — three US hyperscalers account for more than 70% of EU cloud infrastructure, and the EU's own provider share has not recovered in four years. The four assurance levels are not a restriction on trade. They are a classification system designed to make cloud sovereignty a measurable, procurable, and eventually enforceable standard. Organisations that begin mapping their position now will enter the formal assessment process with a documented baseline rather than a blank sheet.
