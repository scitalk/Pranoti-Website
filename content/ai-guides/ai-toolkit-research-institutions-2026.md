The AI toolkit for research institutions in 2026 has five tools, not one. Each tool does a distinct job. NotebookLM combines your own documents into one summary. Claude writes text and creates visual outputs. ChatGPT gives broad task coverage and flexible integrations. Gemini does autonomous web research and creates visuals from data. Napkin.ai turns paragraphs into diagrams. Together, these tools cover the full knowledge work cycle from intake to output, and you do not need coding skills to use them.

## What you need before starting

- **NotebookLM:** Google account (free); NotebookLM Plus available via Google One AI Premium (~€22/month)
- **Claude:** Free account works for light use; Pro plan (€18/month) recommended for consistent institutional workflows and MCP connections; Claude Desktop app required for MCP features
- **ChatGPT:** Free account or Plus plan ($20/month) for GPT-4o access, memory, and the GPT Store
- **Gemini:** Google account; Gemini Advanced (included in Google One AI Premium) for Deep Research and Nano Banana access
- **Napkin.ai:** Free account (500 AI credits/week); Plus ($9/month) for unlimited exports

You do not need command-line experience for any of these tools.

## Five tools, five jobs

| Tool | Primary job |
|---|---|
| NotebookLM | Combine and query your own source documents |
| Claude | Write, edit, and produce formatted visual outputs |
| ChatGPT | General-purpose tasks, broad integrations, memory |
| Gemini | Autonomous web research and data visuals |
| Napkin.ai | Generate diagrams and frameworks from text |

Some overlap between these tools exists, but you can manage it. The sections below show where each tool performs best for institutional knowledge work.

## Synthesising your own documents: NotebookLM

NotebookLM has one constraint: it only knows what you give it. Upload PDFs, Google Docs, audio files, or website URLs (up to 50 sources per notebook). NotebookLM then answers questions using only those materials, and it cites the exact passage for each answer.

For research institutions, this changes how staff do document-heavy work. A grant writer can upload five previous applications and ask which sections were consistently strong. A research manager can upload an evaluation framework and a batch of progress reports, then ask which projects are off track. A policy analyst can load a set of consultation responses and find the key themes without reading every document in full.

The **Audio Overview** feature creates a conversational summary of your sources. Use it to brief colleagues who need context quickly, without asking them to read the full set.

NotebookLM has two limits. It does not search the web. It cannot write polished long-form text. Use it as a synthesis and retrieval tool, not a writing assistant.

## Writing, editing, and visual outputs: Claude

Claude is the writing layer of the institutional AI stack. It drafts text, restructures text, edits for tone, and shortens text without loss of meaning. It also handles the structural work of grant narratives, progress reports, executive summaries, and policy briefs.

Anthropic launched **Claude Design** in April 2026. This feature adds formatted visual outputs. Describe what you need, and Claude Design creates a one-pager, slide deck, or structured document as a designed artifact, not a plain text draft. You can export outputs as PDF, share them by URL, or bring them into Canva or PowerPoint. For institutions that need to present findings visually but have no design team, this closes a real gap.

**MCP** (Model Context Protocol) is the third capability that matters for institutions. MCP connects Claude to your actual files and systems. With MCP active, Claude can read a Google Sheet directly, update a document, or pull data from a connected source, so you do not need to copy and paste data across tools. For teams that run repeatable workflows (monthly reporting, grant tracking, data dashboards), MCP makes Claude a working part of the process, not a one-off assistant.

> MCP connections need the **Claude Desktop** application and a Pro plan or higher. As your connected workflows grow, keep a log of which MCP servers you have active and what each one does. This log stops your setup from going stale. For the pattern, see [Claude Skills Registry: Why Your Automation Library Needs One](/perspectives/claude-skills-registry-and-audit/).

## Broad versatility and integrations: ChatGPT

ChatGPT's strength in an institutional context is breadth. It handles a wider range of task types than any single specialist tool: data analysis, document Q&A, coding assistance, image interpretation, and structured formatting. Its **memory** feature lets it keep context about your projects and preferences across sessions.

The **GPT Store** gives access to purpose-built tools for specific workflows: citation managers, grant-writing assistants, slide builders, and domain-specific research helpers. For institutions where staff have varied needs, ChatGPT's general design is an asset, not a limitation.

ChatGPT also offers **Tasks**, a lightweight scheduling feature. Use Tasks to set recurring prompts for weekly summaries, monitoring tasks, or regular report generation.

ChatGPT fits best in the stack for two cases: tasks with no better specialist option, and tasks that need broad integration with other tools.

## Autonomous web research: Gemini Deep Research + Nano Banana

Gemini's **Deep Research** feature does a task that none of the other tools on this list do: it runs a multi-step, multi-source web research task without supervision. Give it a research question. Deep Research then builds a plan, searches across dozens of sources, combines the findings, and returns a structured report. For a substantive question, this typically takes five to ten minutes.

Deep Research compresses hours of desk research into a single prompt. This helps grant writers who track funding trends, policy analysts who monitor regulatory developments, and research managers who compare their institution against peers.

**Nano Banana** is Gemini's image generation model. It adds visual output to the research capability. Deep Research finds facts, and Nano Banana can render them as conceptual diagrams, illustrative visuals, and data-driven images for presentations and reports. This combination makes Gemini a strong choice for research communication aimed at external audiences.

Both features need Gemini Advanced (included in Google One AI Premium).

## Quick diagrams and frameworks from text: Napkin.ai

Napkin.ai takes a paragraph of text and generates a visual: flowcharts, timelines, mind maps, process diagrams, or comparison frameworks. You can edit the output, and you can export it as PNG, PDF, SVG, or PowerPoint. You do not need any design skill to produce it.

For research institutions, the most practical use cases are:

- **Process maps**: show a multi-step workflow or methodology for a grant application
- **Stakeholder maps**: show relationships between funders, partners, and delivery teams
- **Framework diagrams**: show evaluation models, theory of change, or logic frameworks
- **Report graphics**: replace text-heavy explanations with a single clear visual

The free tier (500 AI credits per week) is enough for most institutional users who need occasional visuals, not a continuous design workflow. The Plus plan removes that limit for teams with higher volume.

## Connecting the stack: MCP and the skills registry

As these tools become part of institutional workflows, the question changes. It moves from "which tools do we use" to "how do we keep them working together reliably."

MCP (the Model Context Protocol) answers this question for the Claude layer of the stack. It connects Claude directly to files, spreadsheets, databases, and publishing systems, and this closes the loop between where data lives and where analysis happens. The [Model Context Protocol primer](/ai-guides/model-context-protocol-non-developers/) explains what MCP is and how to install it without technical experience.

As MCP connections multiply, keep a log of which servers are active, what each one does, and when you last tested it. This log stops the stack from breaking quietly. This practice is the skills registry pattern, a lightweight maintenance habit that scales with the number of connected workflows.

## Building the stack for your institution

The five tools above are not a fixed setup. They are options mapped to jobs. The right starting point depends on what your institution does most.

**If your heaviest work is document synthesis** (reading, summarizing, and querying large sets of reports, papers, or consultation responses), start with NotebookLM. It handles this job better than any general-purpose model.

**If your output load is high** (grant narratives, progress reports, policy briefs, formatted presentations), start with Claude. Add Claude Design for visual outputs. Add MCP when workflows become repeatable.

**If your team needs breadth and has varied task types**, use ChatGPT. Its general design and GPT Store integrations cover more ground than any specialist tool.

**If web research and external monitoring are central to the role**, use Gemini Deep Research. It is the most capable tool on the market for autonomous multi-source research.

**If your work involves communication of findings to non-specialist audiences**, use Napkin.ai. It removes the design bottleneck from visual communication, and you do not need a budget or a designer.

Most institutions will use three or four of these tools, each in its own lane. The goal is a stack where no tool does a job that another tool does better, and where staff log and maintain the connections between them.
